Learning Biological Trajectories with Context-Conditioned CTMCs

Jihwan Kim · Jaeyeon Kim · Sunwoo Kim

Working project · August 29, 2026

Biological sequence data reveal evolutionary endpoints but rarely the edits connecting them. We aim to learn these hidden dynamics as a context-conditioned continuous-time Markov chain over variable-length protein sequences.

Because intermediate trajectories and biological context are unobserved, the framework combines endpoint-guided path sampling with importance correction and contrastive context learning.

1Problem formulation

Our goal is to learn the evolutionary mechanism underlying observed biological sequences.

Evolution may vary across biological settings such as patient, viral clade, antibody family, or experimental condition. We represent the persistent features of such a setting by a latent biological context zzz. Conditioned on zzz, we assume that evolution is governed by an unknown continuous-time Markov chain with generator Q⋆zQ_\star^zQ⋆z​. Starting from sequence xxx, the descendant observed after elapsed biological time Δ\DeltaΔ follows

y∼exp⁡ ⁣(ΔQ⋆z)(x,⋅).y\sim \exp\!\left(\Delta Q_\star^z\right)(x,\cdot).y∼exp(ΔQ⋆z​)(x,⋅).

We approximate the unknown generator Q⋆zQ_\star^zQ⋆z​ with a context-conditioned neural generator QθzQ_\theta^zQθz​:

Qθz≈Q⋆z.Q_\theta^z\approx Q_\star^z.Qθz​≈Q⋆z​.

In practice, the records provide a parent or ancestor sequence xxx, a descendant yyy, and an observed or phylogenetically estimated time interval Δ\DeltaΔ. Grouped sequences provide evidence about the latent context zzz. The edits between the endpoints, their ordering, and their event times remain unobserved.

This is not an artificial corruption process introduced only to generate samples. Here, Δ\DeltaΔ is biological time and the CTMC dynamics themselves are the object to be learned. Recovering them can reveal plausible intermediate states and which antibody-maturation or viral-escape routes are biologically reachable.

2What existing models miss

Existing approaches capture useful parts of the problem, but not the full biological-time dynamics we seek.

Model familyWhat it models—and what remains missing
PEINT[2]Directly models a time-conditioned descendant distribution pθ(y∣x,Δ)p_\theta(y\mid x,\Delta)pθ​(y∣x,Δ), but does not define an explicit CTMC generator whose rates update at hidden intermediate states or depend on latent biological context.
CoSiNE[1]Learns parent-conditioned neural substitution-rate matrices in biological time. It does not model indels, factorizes evolution across sites, and does not update rates along a latent multi-edit trajectory.
Edit Flow, EvoFlows, FlexFlow[3, 4, 5]Use substitutions, insertions, and deletions for variable-length generation. Their aligned edit paths are not observed biological trajectories, and flow time is an interpolation coordinate rather than elapsed biological time.

Our desired model

  • Explicit biological time
  • Substitutions and block indels
  • Latent biological context
  • Intermediate-state-dependent rates

3A neural CTMC over sequence edits

Elementary edits define a sparse generator on the variable-length sequence space.

From a current sequence xxx, the valid edits include single-residue substitutions, contiguous block insertions, and contiguous block deletions. Each concrete edit eee receives a nonnegative rate rθ(e∣x,z)r_\theta(e\mid x,z)rθ​(e∣x,z):

Qθz(x,y)=∑valid edits ee(x)=yrθ(e∣x,z),y≠x,Qθz(x,x)=−∑valid edits erθ(e∣x,z).\begin{aligned}Q_\theta^z(x,y)&=\sum_{\substack{\text{valid edits }e\\ e(x)=y}}r_\theta(e\mid x,z),&&y\neq x,\\[0.35em]Q_\theta^z(x,x)&=-\sum_{\text{valid edits }e}r_\theta(e\mid x,z).\end{aligned}Qθz​(x,y)Qθz​(x,x)​=valid edits ee(x)=y​∑​rθ​(e∣x,z),=−valid edits e∑​rθ​(e∣x,z).​​y=x,​

A length-ℓ\ellℓ biological indel is one event in nature. We therefore model a contiguous block insertion or deletion as a single elementary event rather than as ℓ\ellℓ unrelated single-residue steps.

Local event-rate parameterization

For an edit of type type⁡(e)\operatorname{type}(e)type(e) at position or insertion slot iii, the network separates the rate of initiating the event from the conditional choice of its content:

rθ(e∣x,z)=λθtype⁡(e)(i∣x,z)  qθtype⁡(e) ⁣(content⁡(e)∣i,x,z).r_\theta(e\mid x,z)=\lambda_\theta^{\operatorname{type}(e)}(i\mid x,z)\;q_\theta^{\operatorname{type}(e)}\!\left(\operatorname{content}(e)\mid i,x,z\right).rθ​(e∣x,z)=λθtype(e)​(i∣x,z)qθtype(e)​(content(e)∣i,x,z).

Substitution headevent rate / new residue

Deletion headevent rate / block length

Insertion headevent rate / block length, inserted sequence

Applying edit eee to xxx produces the next sequence. After every edit, the full new sequence is re-encoded and all valid-edit rates are recomputed. This is the source of intermediate-state dependence.

4Learning from endpoint observations

Only the endpoints are observed, so maximum likelihood must integrate over possible intermediate paths.

For endpoint records {(xb,yb,Δb)}b=1B\{(x_b,y_b,\Delta_b)\}_{b=1}^B{(xb​,yb​,Δb​)}b=1B​—suppressing the context variable for readability—the objective is

max⁡θ  ∑b=1Blog⁡ ⁣[exp⁡ ⁣(ΔbQθ)]xb,yb.\max_\theta\;\sum_{b=1}^{B}\log\!\left[\exp\!\left(\Delta_b Q_\theta\right)\right]_{x_b,y_b}.θmax​b=1∑B​log[exp(Δb​Qθ​)]xb​,yb​​.

A latent trajectory may contain multiple intermediate edits, x=x0→e1x1→e2⋯→emxm=yx=x_0\xrightarrow{e_1}x_1\xrightarrow{e_2}\cdots\xrightarrow{e_m}x_m=yx=x0​e1​​x1​e2​​⋯em​​xm​=y. Here, pQθ(ω)p_{Q_\theta}(\omega)pQθ​​(ω) denotes the likelihood of the complete path ω\omegaω under the CTMC with generator QθQ_\thetaQθ​. Fisher's identity converts the endpoint-likelihood gradient into an expectation over the bridge distribution induced by the current CTMC:

∇θlog⁡ ⁣[exp⁡ ⁣(ΔQθ)]x,y=Eω∼path distribution from Qθ ⁣[∇θlog⁡pQθ(ω)].\nabla_\theta\log\!\left[\exp\!\left(\Delta Q_\theta\right)\right]_{x,y}=\mathbb E_{\omega\sim\text{path distribution from }Q_\theta}\!\left[\nabla_\theta\log p_{Q_\theta}(\omega)\right].∇θ​log[exp(ΔQθ​)]x,y​=Eω∼path distribution from Qθ​​[∇θ​logpQθ​​(ω)].

The ideal estimator would therefore sample hidden paths from the QθQ_\thetaQθ​-induced bridge. Direct on-policy simulation is ineffective, however, because only a tiny fraction of paths land exactly at the endpoint yyy at time Δ\DeltaΔ.

For each endpoint record (x,y,Δ)(x,y,\Delta)(x,y,Δ), we generate candidate paths from a proposal that mixes the current CTMC with an endpoint guide HHH:

ω1,…,ωS∼(1−α)Qθ+αH,\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_S\sim(1-\alpha)Q_\theta+\alpha H,ω1​,…,ωS​∼(1−α)Qθ​+αH,

where HHH guides paths toward the endpoint yyy. For each proposed path, we compute the importance weight

w~r=1{ωr(Δ)=y} pQθ(ωr)p(1−α)Qθ+αH(ωr).\widetilde w_r=\mathbf 1\{\omega_r(\Delta)=y\}\,\frac{p_{Q_\theta}(\omega_r)}{p_{(1-\alpha)Q_\theta+\alpha H}(\omega_r)}.wr​=1{ωr​(Δ)=y}p(1−α)Qθ​+αH​(ωr​)pQθ​​(ωr​)​.

The endpoint log-likelihood gradient is then approximated by

∇θlog⁡ ⁣[exp⁡ ⁣(ΔQθ)]x,y≈∑r=1Sw~r ∇θlog⁡pQθ(ωr).\nabla_\theta\log\!\left[\exp\!\left(\Delta Q_\theta\right)\right]_{x,y}\approx\sum_{r=1}^{S}\widetilde w_r\,\nabla_\theta\log p_{Q_\theta}(\omega_r).∇θ​log[exp(ΔQθ​)]x,y​≈r=1∑S​wr​∇θ​logpQθ​​(ωr​).

The guide increases the endpoint hit rate but distorts the sampled path distribution. Importance weighting corrects that proposal bias, so the weighted update still targets the bridge induced by the original QθQ_\thetaQθ​. The guide is a computational device—not part of the learned evolutionary mechanism.

5Learning biological context from grouped sequences

Dense time series are limited, but sequences sharing a patient, viral clade, antibody family, or experimental condition are abundant.

A permutation-invariant context encoder maps grouped sequences to a latent vector zzz. For each group ggg, we randomly divide its sequence collection into two disjoint views, SgA\mathcal S_g^ASgA​ and SgB\mathcal S_g^BSgB​. The same encoder maps both views to zgAz_g^AzgA​ and zgBz_g^BzgB​, forming a positive pair. Encoded sets from any different group h≠gh\neq gh=g provide negatives.

Lctx=−log⁡exp⁡ ⁣(sim⁡(zgA,zgB)/τ)exp⁡ ⁣(sim⁡(zgA,zgB)/τ)+∑h≠gexp⁡ ⁣(sim⁡(zgA,zh)/τ).\mathcal L_{\mathrm{ctx}}=-\log\frac{\exp\!\big(\operatorname{sim}(z_g^A,z_g^B)/\tau\big)}{\exp\!\big(\operatorname{sim}(z_g^A,z_g^B)/\tau\big)+\sum_{h\neq g}\exp\!\big(\operatorname{sim}(z_g^A,z_h)/\tau\big)}.Lctx​=−logexp(sim(zgA​,zgB​)/τ)+∑h=g​exp(sim(zgA​,zh​)/τ)exp(sim(zgA​,zgB​)/τ)​.

The contrastive term makes the representation persistent across different subsets of the same group. We jointly optimize this contrastive objective with the endpoint likelihood. The group context enters the edit-rate model through rθ(e∣x,zg)r_\theta(e\mid x,z_g)rθ​(e∣x,zg​), so the same current sequence may receive different edit rates under different biological contexts.

6Experimental plan

Biological domains and public data

01

Antibody lineage evolution

Learning the dynamics and intermediate states of antibody maturation can reveal which mature antibodies are biologically reachable. Liao et al. reconstructed the CH103 lineage with inferred intermediates[6], and Observed Antibody Space contains 1.54 billion antibody sequences[7].

02

Viral evolution and immune escape

Predicting emerging variants and plausible intermediate states could support earlier surveillance and vaccine updates. EVEscape demonstrated prospective viral-escape forecasting from prepandemic information[8], and VirJenDB contains 15.4 million viral sequences[9].

Baselines

  1. Edit-flow-style modelsEdit-based variable-length generation, including Edit Flow, EvoFlows, and FlexFlow[3, 4, 5].
  2. CoSiNE-style modelsNeural evolutionary models based on site-wise substitution rates[1].
  3. Sequence-supervision modelsAutoregressive and protein language models, mutation predictors, and direct endpoint models such as PEINT[2].

Evaluation

  1. Likelihood-style evaluationHeld-out endpoint likelihood under observed biological time intervals.
  2. Comparison with wet-lab measurementsAgreement between model-induced scores and experimental fitness measurements.
  3. Prediction-style evaluationMutation prediction and forecasting of future prevalent variants.
